Our mission

Preventable diseases are still here.
The data should not be hard to read.

Measles, rubella and pertussis are all preventable by vaccines that have existed for decades. They still infect hundreds of thousands of people every year. This site takes the World Health Organization's reported figures for {{ scope.first_year }}–{{ scope.last_year }} and makes them comparable — between countries of very different sizes, and between countries of very different means — without arguing a case for you.

How this site addresses the challenge

Vaccination is one of the few interventions where the evidence is settled and the remaining problem is distribution — who gets reached, and who does not. Yet the public record of that problem is published as raw case counts, which are close to useless for comparison. A country reporting 8,000 measles cases sounds far worse than one reporting 900, until you learn the first has 200 million people and the second has 90,000.

Our position is that comparison requires a denominator. Every figure on this site that invites a comparison is expressed per 100,000 people, calculated from that country's population in that specific year. The raw count and the population are always shown next to the rate, so you can check the arithmetic rather than trust it.

We also show what we excluded. Of the {{ scope.n_infection_rows | num }} infection records supplied, {{ quality.countries_no_infection_data }} countries in the reference list report no infection data at all, and {{ quality.orphan_countries }} country codes appear in the vaccination records with no matching country. Those rows are dropped rather than counted as zero, because a country that did not report is not the same as a country with no cases. Treating silence as success is the single most common way immunisation data gets misread.

What we do not do

  • We do not rank countries into league tables of “good” and “bad”.
  • We do not present reported cases as true incidence.
  • We do not fill gaps in the data with estimates or interpolation.
  • We do not editorialise on any country's health policy.

Where a figure could be read more than one way — such as a global average — we show both readings and name the difference.

How to use this site

Three pages, arranged from the broadest view to the most specific question. You do not need a statistics background for any of them.

  1. 1

    Start here, on the mission page

    Find out what data the site holds, over what period, and what its limits are. If you only read one thing before looking at a number, read the “What we do not do” panel above.

  2. 2

    Compare disease burden by economic status

    On Infection & Economy, pick one income group, one disease and one year. You get every country in that group with its case count, its population and its rate per 100,000 — and underneath, all four income groups side by side, so you can see whether the pattern you are looking at is specific to that group or general.

    Sort any column by clicking its heading.

  3. 3

    Find who is above the global rate

    On Above the Global Rate, pick a disease and a year. The site works out the global infection rate per 100,000 people, pins it to the top of the table, and lists only the countries that exceed it — sorted however you choose, including by how close a country is to the global figure.
